The slow half of the average

Run the first 5 km of a 10 km race at 12 km/h and the second 5 km at 10 km/h. Your average speed is not 11. It is 10.9.

The reason is quiet but important. Time, not distance, is what a race charges you, and slow kilometres take longer to run, so they weigh more in the average. Averaged over equal distances, speed is the harmonic mean, which always sits below the ordinary one. Pace, which is time per kilometre, averages the way you expect: 5:00 and 6:00 give 5:30. The two are the same fact in different clothes, and only one of them lets you do the sum in your head.

average speed = 2ab / (a + b) = 2 × 12 × 10 / 22 ≈ 10.91 km/h

There is a deeper version of the same idea. In a simple model where the cost of going faster grows more than proportionally, Jensen's inequality says the cheapest way to cover a distance in a fixed time is to hold the same speed throughout. Surges are expensive, and a fast stretch rarely buys back what a slow one spends. Hills, wind and heat all bend the model, but it is a good first map.

What I like is that running makes this felt rather than merely known. You can solve it on paper in a minute and still learn it properly at kilometre eight, when the early enthusiasm sends its invoice. The habit I take from it is a small one: when a plan seems to need a heroic stretch to work, I check whether steady effort does the same job.
